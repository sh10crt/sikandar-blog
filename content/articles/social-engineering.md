# Social Engineering: The Human Side of Cybersecurity

Cybersecurity is not only about computers, networks and software.

Sometimes, the weakest point in a security system is the person sitting in front of the screen.

This is where **social engineering** comes into play.

## What is social engineering?

Social engineering is the use of psychological manipulation to persuade someone to perform an action or reveal information.

Instead of trying to break through a technical security control, an attacker may try to convince a person to open a file, click a link, reveal a password or transfer money.

## Why does social engineering work?

Attackers often take advantage of normal human behaviour.

For example, people naturally respond to:

- Authority
- Urgency
- Fear
- Curiosity
- Trust
- Helpfulness

An attacker may pretend to be a bank employee, a manager, a colleague, a delivery company or even a technical support worker.

The goal is to make the victim believe that the request is legitimate.

## Common social engineering techniques

### Phishing

Phishing usually involves fraudulent emails or messages designed to trick someone into clicking a malicious link or providing sensitive information.

### Vishing

Vishing is voice-based phishing.

An attacker may call a victim and pretend to be from a bank, government organisation or technical support department.

### Smishing

Smishing is phishing through SMS or text messages.

A message might claim that a parcel could not be delivered or that an account requires verification.

### Pretexting

Pretexting involves creating a believable story or scenario to obtain information from a target.

For example, an attacker might pretend to be an employee who has forgotten their login details.

## How can you protect yourself?

The most important defence against social engineering is to slow down and verify unexpected requests.

Before taking action, ask yourself:

1. Was I expecting this message?
2. Is the request creating unnecessary urgency?
3. Is someone asking for sensitive information?
4. Can I verify the request through another trusted method?
5. Does the message contain unusual links, attachments or instructions?

Never assume that a message is legitimate simply because it appears to come from someone you know.

## Final thoughts

Technology can provide strong security controls, but people are still an important part of cybersecurity.

Understanding how attackers manipulate human behaviour can make it much easier to recognise suspicious situations.

Sometimes the best security control is simply taking a moment to stop, think and verify.
